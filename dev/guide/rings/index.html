Ring systems

RingedLimb(body; rings=n) fits a body together with n circular rings. The rings lie in the plane of the ring pole and are centred on the body.

KindParameters
bodyany LimbModel (EllipseLimb, CircleLimb, EllipsoidLimb, …), its parameters unchanged
ring planethe ring pole ring_pole_ra, ring_pole_dec [deg]
ring kmid radius ringk_radius [km], radial width ringk_width [km], normal optical depth ringk_tau

Geometry

On the sky a circular ring is an elliptical annulus, a ProjectedRing. Two angles describe it in each event:

  • the opening angle B: the angle between the line of sight and the ring plane;
  • the position angle P of the projected ring pole.

Its edges are ellipses with semi-axes r ± W/2 and (r ± W/2)|sin B|, with the minor axis along P. The ring is partly transparent. Its apparent opacity is

p′ = 1 − exp(−τ_N / |sin B|),

the fraction of the stellar flux that it blocks.

ring_geometry(pole_ra, pole_dec, u) gives B and P for a line of sight u. project_ring gives the projected ring.

Ambiguities of the ring plane:

  • A pole and its antipode describe the same ring plane. The fitted pole is the one in the northern hemisphere.
  • One event fixes only the ellipse: the radius, |B| and P. The plane and its mirror image (opening angle −B, the pole tilted towards or away from the observer) give the same ellipse.
  • Several events at different geometries decide between the two planes, but only if the line of sight changes enough. For distant bodies it changes by about a degree per year. See the three-event example.

Observations

A ring dip of a chord is a pair of contacts labelled with its ring: the passage of the star through the annulus.

Chord("Station 3", obs; contacts=[
    Contact(:immersion, t1, 0.05; body=:ring1), Contact(:emersion, t2, 0.05; body=:ring1),   # ring, ingress
    Contact(:immersion, t3, 0.03), Contact(:emersion, t4, 0.03),                             # the body
    Contact(:immersion, t5, 0.05; body=:ring1), Contact(:emersion, t6, 0.05; body=:ring1)])  # ring, egress
  • Ring contacts must be labelled (:ring1 … :ring9; :ring is :ring1). Each is compared with the nearest crossing of the same kind of that ring's annulus, along the chord.
  • Unlabelled contacts belong to the body.
  • Negative chords and the visible segments of the positive chords constrain the body only: a ring dip may escape detection.

Ring dips in light curves. A ring dip is short and partly transparent.

  • Finding them: the box search detect_occultations(lc; snr_limit=6, maxdur=3) outside the body drop.
  • Identifying them: by their symmetry about the body and by comparison with the neighbouring chords. A faint ring is seen only in the better light curves, so a blind assignment by order is error-prone.
  • Timing them: with the opacity free, fit_timing(lc; tmin, tmax, fit_opacity=true), in a window between the neighbouring drops.

The two-ring example shows the whole chain, from simulated light curves to the fits.

Which data constrain what:

  • The timings constrain the radii and widths of the rings.
  • The optical depths come from the apparent opacities. They are fitted in the joint fit of the light curves (photometry = true). There the rings are partly transparent strips with diffraction: the complex amplitudes of the strips add, each weighted by its transmission, as for the gaps of a contact binary. The stellar disc and the exposure are included too.
  • Fix the optical depths (fix=[:ring1_tau, …]) in a fit of the timings alone.
  • Where the exposures are comparable to the duration of a ring crossing, the width and the optical depth of a ring are correlated. Their product, the equivalent depth W τ_N, is then the robust quantity.
  • A faint ring is found only in the best light curves. A ring without any detected dip has no data; leave it out of the model.
  • Few, coarse light curves: the covariance errors of the joint fit can be far too small (in the example the ring radius is 0.28 km off at a formal error of 0.017 km); check them with the posterior or with simulations.

Simulation. simulate_lightcurve(t, drops; opacity=[…]) simulates light curves with ring dips, and predicted_contacts(event, observer, ring) predicts ring crossings.

Plots

plot(fit) and plot(posterior) draw:

  • the edges of the rings, in orange;
  • the body;
  • the chords, with each ring dip and each body drop as a segment.

The plot box covers the rings.

Not yet included

  • eccentric or inclined rings, and rings with their own centre;
  • radial profiles within a ring;
  • maps of the ring shadow paths.

Examples

  • Synthetic: a body with two narrow rings, Chariklo-like:
    • light curves with ring dips;
    • detection and timing of the dips;
    • the fit of the timings, then the joint fit of the light curves for the optical depths.
  • Synthetic: a distant ring seen in three events, Quaoar-like:
    • the ring ellipse of each event and its mirror ambiguity;
    • the ring pole from the three events together.
